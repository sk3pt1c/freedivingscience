    Why This Matters for Freedivers

    This paper is a wake-up call: serious decompression illness can happen in breath-hold diving, and some divers are already attempting in-water recompression (IWR) on themselves—sometimes deep, sometimes with major neurological symptoms. Understanding what happened in these real cases helps you make better prevention choices (hydration, pacing, packing, ascent behaviour) and helps you plan the only smart response: oxygen, emergency activation, and proper medical evaluation rather than improvised “fixes” that can add drowning and oxygen-toxicity risk.

    Synopsis

    “In-water recompression” (IWR) is a controversial idea: if a diver surfaces with symptoms that look like decompression illness (DCI), they re-enter the water to increase pressure again, ideally while breathing oxygen, hoping symptoms improve while they arrange definitive treatment. In scuba, IWR has been discussed in limited, very specific circumstances. In freediving, it’s not formally established—and yet it’s already being used in the wild.

    This paper reports a case series built from detailed interviews with six competitive freedivers who described 13 incidents of severe neurological symptoms after breath-hold diving. The authors then tried to classify each incident as likely decompression sickness (DCS), arterial gas embolism (AGE), or ambiguous DCI, and they documented what treatment the divers attempted (surface oxygen, IWR, hyperbaric chamber treatment, or combinations).

    A few patterns jump out immediately. First, these were not “minor headaches” cases: several incidents included stroke-like symptoms such as limb paralysis, facial drooping, aphasia (can’t speak properly), severe dizziness, and abnormal behaviour. Second, most dives were deep (the median deepest dive reported per case was around 100 m), but the paper also includes a notable case with symptoms after repetitive 40 m dives with long hangs, and it highlights that similar syndromes have been described even after shallow breath-hold diving or after lung-packing alone.

    The suspected contributing factors are very familiar to freedivers: - dehydration and exhaustion, - repetitive deep dives with short surface intervals, - heavy lung packing, - exertion after the dive (like hauling the bottom weight), - signs suggesting pulmonary barotrauma in some cases (coughing and even haemoptysis).

    Six of the 13 cases involved IWR as part of self-treatment. The reported IWR “protocols” varied wildly: times ranged from around 20 to 90 minutes, and depths ranged from about 5 m up to 25 m. Some divers used IWR as a bridge until they could reach a hyperbaric chamber, and some used it as the main treatment because a chamber was far away. In several cases, symptoms partially or completely improved after IWR and/or surface oxygen. But the series also contains a hard warning: one diver reported significant lasting disability, and in at least one case there was a long delay to definitive hyperbaric oxygen treatment (HBOT), which is known to reduce the chance of full recovery.

    The paper’s overall tone is not “IWR works, go do it.” It’s closer to: “this is happening; it can be dangerous; and we need proper freediving-specific guidelines.” The authors emphasize that IWR carries serious risks—especially drowning (because these divers are already neurologically impaired), and oxygen toxicity (especially at deeper IWR depths than commonly recommended in scuba guidance). They also point out that many of the symptoms described would normally be considered contraindications to IWR in existing discussions (altered mental status, vomiting, severe physical impairment, respiratory distress).

    The practical bottom line is blunt: deep freediving can produce DCI-like events, divers in remote locations are improvising, and the community needs better prevention strategies and clearer emergency decision-making. If a diver has neurological symptoms after a dive, “wait and see” and “self-experiment in the water” are both risky. Oxygen, urgent medical evaluation, and—when indicated—proper HBOT remain the safest route.

    Abstract

    Introduction: There are increasing anecdotal reports of in-water recompression in freedivers who surface with neurological symptoms, likely suffering from decompression illness (DCI). Given the remote locations where many cases occurred, divers often struggled to access medical care, including the gold-standard hyperbaric oxygen treatment (HBOT), thus resorting to in-water recompression (IWR). Currently, IWR guidelines have only been discussed for scuba and surface supplied divers in specific scenarios, with protocols prescribing oxygen breathing at depths ≤ 9 metres maximum for around 1–3 hours.
    Methods: We conducted detailed interviews with six competitive freedivers on signs, symptoms, management, and resolution of 13 cases of DCI. We additionally requested records of medical evaluation and treatment, with their consent.
    Results: Three cases were suggestive of decompression sickness, six were consistent with arterial gas embolism, and four were ambiguous. Six cases were treated with IWR for 20–90 min at 5–25 metres with partial to complete resolution of symptoms. Four of these cases received HBOT afterwards. One diver reported significant permanent disability. Divers made several regimen changes after these incidents, including staying well-hydrated, reducing lung-packing, slowing their ascent rate, and/or employing prophylactic IWR when diving beyond a specified depth.
    Conclusions: Given the remote locations of many incidents, freedivers often faced challenges in accessing HBOT. Self-treatment with IWR was widely used, either as a bridge to HBOT or as a standalone remedy. IWR poses potential risks, especially at the deeper depths reported in this study. This treatment modality is being utilised sometimes without medical oversight and recommended guidelines for IWR for freedivers should be developed.
