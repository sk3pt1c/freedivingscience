    Reading level: Intermediate

    Why This Matters for Freedivers

    Many freedivers still assume “DCS is only for scuba.” This review explains why that’s not always true—especially with lots of repetitive dives, short surface intervals, and certain deep-dive patterns. It helps you spot which dive styles raise risk, so you can plan sessions more safely and take symptoms seriously instead of brushing them off.

    Synopsis

    Most of us learn early on that decompression sickness (DCS) is a “breathing compressed gas” problem—so it sounds like freedivers should be immune. This review argues that the story is more nuanced. The key idea is simple: nitrogen can still build up in your body during breath-hold diving. One single dive usually isn’t the issue. The risk comes when dives are repeated many times, over hours, with short breaks on the surface—exactly the pattern you see in spearfishing and some training sessions.

    The authors walk through the basic mechanism: nitrogen dissolves into tissues under pressure, and if it comes out too quickly on ascent it can form bubbles. In scuba, the exposure time is long and continuous, so the logic is obvious. In freediving, each exposure is short, but the repetition can create a cumulative effect—especially if the surface interval is too short for nitrogen to wash out. The review also points out that bubbles (and sometimes symptoms) have been reported in breath-hold divers, and that neurological-type symptoms are a recurring theme in case reports.

    A really interesting part of the paper is the comparison with marine mammals. Many mammals do deep, repetitive diving with short surface intervals—yet they usually don’t show classic DCS during normal life. The authors discuss why: things like diving behaviour patterns, and physiology that may limit nitrogen uptake. They also highlight that when behaviour changes (for example, unusual dive patterns), risk might change too.

    For humans, the review suggests that different freediving “styles” may lead to similar problems through different routes: shallow-repetitive diving over many hours, versus single very deep dives. It also discusses protective factors that might reduce nitrogen transfer at depth—like lung collapse and pulmonary shunting—and how these could change the balance between “nitrogen in” and “nitrogen out.” The practical takeaway isn’t fear—it’s awareness: DCS-like illness is plausible in freediving under certain patterns, and the patterns matter.

    Abstract

    Although it has been generally assumed that the risk of decompression sickness is virtually zero during a single breath-hold dive in humans, repeated dives may result in a cumulative increase in the tissue and blood nitrogen tension. Many species of marine mammals perform extensive foraging bouts with deep and long dives interspersed by a short surface interval, and some human divers regularly perform repeated dives to 30–40 m or a single dive to more than 200 m, all of which may result in nitrogen concentrations that elicit symptoms of decompression sickness. Neurological problems have been reported in humans after single or repeated dives and recent necropsy reports in stranded marine mammals were suggestive of decompression sickness-like symptoms. Modelling attempts have suggested that marine mammals may live permanently with elevated nitrogen concentrations and may be at risk when altering their dive behaviour. In humans, non-pathogenic bubbles have been recorded and symptoms of decompression sickness have been reported after repeated dives to modest depths. The mechanisms implicated in these accidents indicate that repeated breath-hold dives with short surface intervals are factors that predispose to decompression sickness. During deep diving, the effect of pulmonary shunts and/or lung collapse may play a major role in reducing the incidence of decompression sickness in humans and marine mammals.
