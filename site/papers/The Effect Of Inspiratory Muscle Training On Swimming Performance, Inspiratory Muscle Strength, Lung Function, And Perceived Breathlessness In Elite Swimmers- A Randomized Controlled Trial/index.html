    Reading level: Intermediate

    Why This Matters for Freedivers

    IMT is popular in freediving, but this study is a good reminder that “stronger breathing muscles” doesn’t always translate into better sport performance—especially in athletes who already train their breathing hard as part of the sport. It’s useful for setting expectations: IMT might help some people, but it’s not guaranteed, and training load + compliance matter a lot.

    Synopsis

    Inspiratory muscle training (IMT) is marketed like strength training for your “inhale muscles,” usually using a handheld device that makes it harder to breathe in. In many land sports, IMT has been linked to better performance and less breathlessness. But swimmers are a special case: they already train under conditions that challenge breathing—water pressure on the chest, limited breathing opportunities, and high ventilation during hard sets. So the big question is: if you add IMT to elite swimming training, do you actually get anything extra?

    This trial tested that idea in elite swimmers from a top club team. Swimmers were randomly split into two groups for 12 weeks. Both groups did the same IMT routine (30 strong inhalations, twice per day, five days per week), but with very different resistance: the “real” IMT group trained at a higher load (about 50% of their maximal inspiratory pressure), while the control group trained at a low load (about 15%). The researchers measured swimming performance using a 200 m time trial converted into FINA points, plus inspiratory strength (maximal inspiratory pressure), spirometry (FVC, FEV1, peak flow), and how breathless swimmers felt after the trial (Borg scale).

    The punchline is simple: after 12 weeks, there were no meaningful differences between the high-load IMT group and the low-load control group for performance, inspiratory strength, lung function, or perceived breathlessness. Both groups improved a bit over time, but they improved similarly—suggesting the added IMT didn’t produce a clear extra benefit beyond normal swimming training (and/or beyond the placebo/low-load effect).

    A key practical detail is compliance: athletes completed far fewer IMT days than planned, especially as competitions increased. That matters because IMT works like any training—if you don’t do enough of it, or the load isn’t progressed enough, you may not get an adaptation. The authors also point out a bigger idea: elite swimmers may already be near a “ceiling” for respiratory muscle function because swimming itself is a heavy respiratory workout.

    For freedivers, this doesn’t mean IMT is useless. It means context matters: highly trained athletes in a breathing-demanding sport may not gain much from a modest IMT add-on. If you use IMT, think in training terms—progressive overload, consistency, and clear goals—rather than assuming it automatically boosts performance.

    Abstract

    Background: According to studies performed on terrestrial sports athletes, inspiratory muscle training (IMT) may improve athletes’ performance. However, evidence of its effects in elite swimmers is lacking. Therefore, we aimed to assess the effect of 12-week IMT on swimming performance, inspiratory muscle strength, lung function, and perceived breathlessness in elite swimmers.
    Methods: Elite swimmers from the main FC Porto swimming team (in competitive training for a minimum period of 3 years) were invited to participate and were randomly allocated into intervention or control groups. The intervention group performed 30 inspiratory efforts, twice a day, 5 times a week, against a pressure threshold load equivalent to 50% of maximal inspiratory pressure, whereas the control group performed inspiratory efforts at the same frequency but against a 15% load. Swimming performance was assessed through time trials, converted into points according to International Swimming Federation Points Table. Outcomes were evaluated before and following the 12-week study period.
    Results: A total of 32 participants (22 girls) were included. The median age was 15 and 14 years old for the intervention (n=17) and control (n=12) groups, respectively. No differences were found in swimming performance, inspiratory muscle strength, forced vital capacity, forced expiratory volume in 1st second, peak expiratory flow, and perceived breathlessness between groups after 12 weeks of intervention.
    Conclusion: Twelve weeks of IMT had no effect on swimming performance, lung function, and perceived breathlessness in elite swimmers. These results may be related to swimming-specific factors and/or an applied load insufficient to achieve training overload that could induce further improvements.
