## Why This Matters for Freedivers
Dynamic is where freediving turns into a real “oxygen budgeting” problem: you’re spending oxygen to move while trying to stay calm enough to conserve it. This paper gives you a practical map of what actually drives distance (technique, efficiency, anaerobic capacity, and brain hypoxia risk), plus a clear explanation of why pool safety protocols focus so hard on the seconds right after surfacing.

## Synopsis
This is a broad, freediver-friendly review focused on the pool distance disciplines: **dynamic with fins (DYN)** and **dynamic no fins (DNF)**. It builds on Part I (static apnea) and asks: what changes when you add real physical work and still try to push a single maximal attempt.

The core message is that dynamic performance isn’t just “how long you can hold your breath.” Static ability still matters (big oxygen stores, CO₂ tolerance, low resting metabolism), but in dynamic the main challenge becomes **doing useful work with the smallest possible oxygen cost**, while keeping the brain oxygenated enough to avoid hypoxic loss of motor control or blackout.

A few big ideas the paper pulls together:

- **Work economy is king.** Technique, streamlining, correct weighting, and choosing an efficient speed matter enormously because drag rises fast with speed. In DYN (usually monofin at elite level), propulsion is mostly legs and trunk with glide phases; in DNF, the whole body works more and distances tend to be shorter, suggesting higher energy cost.

- **Blood flow is a negotiation.** The diving response still helps by lowering heart rate and restricting blood flow to “non-essential” areas, but in dynamic you also need blood to working muscles. The big question becomes what fails first: muscle power (local hypoxia/fatigue) or brain oxygenation (syncope). The author argues brain oxygenation is often the limiting safety factor, especially in DNF where syncope frequency has been high in competition.

- **Anaerobic contribution is huge.** Very high lactate values seen after competition dynamics suggest that even though the swimming can look smooth and controlled, the body is leaning heavily on anaerobic metabolism during maximal efforts. That helps you keep moving when oxygen is low, but it also creates a major recovery cost.

- **Preparation strategies vary—and not everything is proven.** The paper discusses breathe-up styles (relaxation breathing vs hyperventilation), lung packing, warm-up vs no warm-up, and even fasting vs eating before events. A key point is that top divers often use very different strategies, and what helps one diver may limit another.

- **Equipment and technique have driven record progression.** Monofins, better weighting systems, low-drag suits, goggles/nose clips, shaving/caps—small gains matter when oxygen is fixed.

- **Safety in pool disciplines can be kept high—if protocols are strict.** The paper explains the logic behind competition safety: safety divers shadow the athlete closely, and the surface protocol (remove facial equipment, OK sign, verbal “I am OK” within a short window) is designed to catch hypoxia in the critical post-surfacing phase when oxygen saturation can keep dropping for a moment. It also mentions common recovery techniques like hook breathing and outlines rescue priorities.

Overall, it’s an excellent “big picture” guide to what actually predicts dynamic distance: **efficient movement + controlled speed + strong apnea base + enough anaerobic capacity to keep propulsion going**, all while managing the real risk—**brain hypoxia near the end and immediately after surfacing**.

## Abstract
Part I of this series of articles identified the main physiological factors defining the limits of static apnea, while this paper reviews the factors involved when physical work is added in the dynamic distance disciplines, performed in shallow water in a swimming pool. Little scientific work has been done concerning the prerequisites and limitations of swimming with or without fins whilst breath-holding to extreme limits. Apneic duration influences all competitive apnea disciplines, and can be prolonged by any means that increase gas storage or tolerance to asphyxia, or reduce metabolic rate, as reviewed in the first article. For horizontal underwater distance swimming, the main challenge is to restrict metabolism despite the work, and to direct blood flow only to areas where demand is greatest, to allow sustained function. Here, work economy, local tissue energy and oxygen stores and the anaerobic capacity of the muscles are key components. Improvements in swimming techniques and, especially in swimming with fins, equipment have already contributed to enhanced performance and may do so further. High lactate levels observed after dynamic competition dives suggest a high anaerobic component, and muscle hypoxia could ultimately limit muscle work and swimming distance. However, the frequency of syncope, especially in swimming without fins, suggests that cerebral oxygenation may often be compromised before this occurs. In these pool disciplines, safety is high and the dive can be interrupted by the competitor or safety diver within seconds. The safety routines in place during pool competitions are described.
