> **Reading level:** Intermediate

## Why This Matters for Freedivers
Freediving regularly exposes the brain and body to **hypoxia** and intense physiological stress, and this review highlights research suggesting DMT may interact with systems involved in **stress response and cellular survival** (notably sigma-1 receptors). Even if the “DMT experience” isn’t a freediving topic, understanding what science *does* and *doesn’t* know about endogenous DMT helps keep discussions about dreams, near-death experiences, and hypoxia grounded in evidence.  

## Synopsis
This paper is a deep but readable tour of one of neuroscience’s strangest questions: if DMT is a powerful hallucinogen when taken from the outside, what does it mean that the body may also produce it naturally? Barker reviews decades of research on **where DMT might come from in the body**, how it’s **broken down**, and where it has been **detected** (blood, urine, CSF, and limited animal brain data). A major theme is that the science is messy: early studies often had weak detection methods, and we still don’t have solid maps of DMT levels in specific human brain regions.  

The review then shifts from “is it there?” to “what could it do?” It summarizes DMT’s known receptor targets (especially **serotonin receptors like 5-HT2A**, but also **trace amine-associated receptors** and **sigma-1 receptors**), and argues that focusing on a single receptor probably won’t explain everything. One of the most intriguing modern directions discussed is sigma-1: DMT may act as a regulator in pathways linked to **cell stress, oxidative stress, and hypoxia survival**, which opens the door to non-mystical roles for an “endogenous hallucinogen” (e.g., neuroprotection or signaling during extreme physiology). The paper ends by proposing concrete next steps: better brain mapping of the enzymes involved (INMT/AADC), clearer work on the pineal gland, improved measurement of DMT + metabolites, and careful separation of “natural role” from the effects of external dosing.  

## Abstract
This report provides a historical overview of research concerning the endogenous hallucinogen N, N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT), focusing on data regarding its biosynthesis and metabolism in the brain and peripheral tissues, methods and results for DMT detection in body fluids and brain, new sites of action for DMT, and new data regarding its possible physiological and therapeutic roles. Research that further elaborates its consideration as a putative neurotransmitter is also addressed. Taking these studies together, the report proposes several new directions and experiments to ascertain the role of DMT in the brain, including brain mapping of enzymes responsible for the biosynthesis of DMT, further studies to elaborate its presence and role in the pineal gland, a reconsideration of binding site data, and new administration and imaging studies. The need to resolve the “natural” role of an endogenous hallucinogen from the effects observed from peripheral administration are also emphasized.
