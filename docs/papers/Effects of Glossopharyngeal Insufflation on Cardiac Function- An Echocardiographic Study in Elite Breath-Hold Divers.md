## Why This Matters for Freedivers
This paper is a big warning label for heavy packing on land: in elite divers, lung packing triggered a dramatic blood pressure drop and major changes in heart function within seconds. It explains why some divers feel dizzy or even black out from packing—especially when standing—and why packing should be treated like an advanced tool, not a casual habit.

## Synopsis
Lung packing (glossopharyngeal insufflation) is used by some freedivers to push lung volume above normal total lung capacity. It can help with equalisation and depth, but divers have reported feeling light-headed or even losing consciousness during packing. This study asked a simple question: is the blood pressure drop just “less blood returning to the heart,” or is the heart itself temporarily failing to pump properly during packing?

To answer that, the authors performed transthoracic echocardiography (heart ultrasound) in five elite breath-hold divers—at rest, and then during a short bout of packing (about 15–30 mouth “pumps” over ~30 seconds). Because the lungs create ultrasound artifacts during packing, the measurements were taken from a subcostal view (under the ribs), which still allowed them to estimate heart chamber sizes, volumes, and ejection fractions.

The results were extreme. During packing, every diver’s heart rate jumped (on average from about 53 bpm to about 100 bpm), while blood pressure collapsed. Average systolic pressure fell from around 112 mmHg to about 52 mmHg, and diastolic pressure became undetectable by cuff measurement. That kind of drop is fully capable of causing near-fainting or fainting on its own.

At the same time, the heart changed shape and performance in a very specific way. The left ventricle (the main pump that sends blood to the body) filled much less: the average left-ventricular end-diastolic volume fell by roughly 70%. Meanwhile, the right ventricle tended to enlarge and fill more, consistent with the right side struggling against a sudden rise in pressure in the lung circulation. Both sides showed reduced pumping performance: the left-ventricular ejection fraction roughly halved (from about 0.60 down to about 0.30), and the right-ventricular ejection fraction also dropped sharply (from about 0.75 to about 0.39). Wall motion became abnormal—especially in the interventricular septum—suggesting the two ventricles were mechanically interfering with each other (“ventricular interdependence”).

In two divers, the inferior vena cava dilated and showed “spontaneous contrast,” a sign of sluggish venous flow and venous stasis during packing. That supports the idea that venous return is impaired and blood is pooling upstream while the heart is also dealing with unfavorable pressures and geometry.

The authors interpret the pattern as acute right-ventricular pressure overload (from increased pulmonary vascular resistance and transmitted intrathoracic pressure), with secondary effects on the left ventricle due to reduced filling and mechanical distortion. They also point out that these measurements were done in air; immersion could partly blunt the effect by shifting blood centrally and limiting extreme lung expansion. But the headline message remains: on land, packing can acutely and severely disrupt circulation and heart function, and the safety of the practice—especially heavy packing in the non-immersed state—needs caution.

## Abstract
Glossopharyngeal insufflation (GI), a technique used by breath-hold divers to increase lung volume and augment diving depth and duration, is associated with untoward hemodynamic consequences. To study the cardiac effects of GI, we performed transthoracic echocardiography, using the subcostal window, in five elite breath-hold divers at rest and during GI. During GI, heart rate increased in all divers, and blood pressure fell dramatically. GI induced a marked decrease in left ventricular end-diastolic size and volume and increases in right ventricular size and volume. GI also induced biventricular systolic dysfunction with reduced ejection fractions and abnormal wall motion, particularly involving the interventricular septum. In two divers, the inferior vena cava dilated with spontaneous contrast during GI, signaling increased right atrial pressure and central venous stasis. Hypotension during GI is associated with acute biventricular systolic dysfunction. The echocardiographic pattern of right ventricular systolic dysfunction is consistent with acute pressure overload, whereas concurrent left ventricular systolic dysfunction is likely due to ventricular interdependence.
