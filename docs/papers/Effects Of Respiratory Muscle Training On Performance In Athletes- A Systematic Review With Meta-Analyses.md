## Why This Matters for Freedivers
Freediving performance is often limited by how efficiently you can manage breathing work before and between dives, and how well your breathing muscles cope with fatigue. This review suggests that targeted respiratory muscle training can improve performance in athletes, which supports the idea that training the “breathing engine” (not just legs and lungs) can give real benefits—especially for disciplines where discomfort and fatigue build fast.

## Synopsis
Most athletes spend years training their heart, legs, and technique—but the breathing muscles (mainly the diaphragm and muscles around the ribs) often get ignored. The idea behind respiratory muscle training is simple: if the muscles that pull air in and out get stronger and more resistant to fatigue, you might breathe more easily under load, feel less “air hunger,” and keep performance steadier when things get intense.

This paper pulls together the available research in a structured way. The authors searched major databases and only included controlled trials where athletes did a form of respiratory muscle training and were compared to a sham or control group. They looked at two big categories of outcomes: (1) sports performance (for example, time trials and endurance-style tests) and (2) respiratory muscle function (strength and endurance of the breathing muscles).

Across the included studies, the overall pattern was that respiratory muscle training can improve sports performance in certain kinds of tests. The strongest signals showed up in outcomes like time trials, endurance time, and repeated-effort tests (like Yo-Yo style tests). At the same time, many studies also showed improvements in inspiratory muscle strength and endurance—meaning the “inhale muscles” could generate more pressure and sustain work longer.

A key practical point is that not all respiratory training is the same. Different methods were used (for example, threshold loading devices vs. high-ventilation “hyperpnea” training), programs varied in length, and training progression wasn’t always aggressive. The authors argue that results are likely better when the training matches the ventilatory demands of the sport: the speed and depth of breathing, and how hard the breathing muscles must work during competition. They also highlight a limitation across the field: sample sizes are often small, and testing methods vary, which makes it harder to say exactly who benefits most (elite vs recreational, endurance vs power sports, and so on).

Takeaway: respiratory muscle training isn’t magic, but the evidence suggests it can produce meaningful gains—especially when it’s progressed properly and designed to resemble the breathing demands of real performance.

## Abstract
The purpose of this study was to perform a systematic review to determine if respiratory muscle training (RMT) improves sport performance and respiratory muscle strength and endurance. Methodology followed the Cochrane Collaboration protocol. MEDLINE, CINAHL, SPORTDiscus, PEDro, EMBASE, EBM reviews, and COCHRANE electronic databases were searched until July 2011. Articles were included if: (a) participants were athletes; (b) RMT was compared with sham or control in a randomized controlled design and included outcomes of respiratory muscle and sport performance; and (d) published in English. Quality assessment using PEDro and data abstraction was performed by 2 authors. Outcomes evaluated were measures of sport performance, exercise capacity, spirometry, and respiratory muscle strength and endurance. Meta-analyses were performed on outcomes reported in 2 or more papers. Results of this systematic review revealed that of the 6,923 citations retrieved from the search strategy, 21 met the inclusion criteria. Meta-analyses demonstrated a significant positive effect of RMT on sport performance outcomes of time trials, exercise endurance time, and repetitions on Yo-Yo tests. Inspiratory muscle strength and endurance improved in most studies, which in part, was dependent on the type of RMT employed. Determination of the type of athlete that may benefit most from RMT was limited by small sample sizes, differing RMT protocols, and differences in outcome measures across studies. In conclusion, RMT can improve sport performance. Closer attention to matching the ventilatory demands of RMT to those required during athletic competition and more aggressive progression of training intensity may show greater improvements in future studies.
