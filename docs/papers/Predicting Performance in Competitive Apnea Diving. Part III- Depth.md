## Why This Matters for Freedivers
Depth freediving isn’t “just longer apnea”—pressure adds extra risks like squeeze, narcosis-like effects, and decompression problems that change how you plan training, ascent speed, and safety. This review helps you understand what actually limits depth performance and where the real danger points are, especially when you can’t simply stop and breathe.

## Synopsis
This paper is the third part of a review series on what predicts performance in competitive freediving. The earlier parts focused on static apnea (time) and dynamic apnea (distance). Here, the author explains what changes when you add **depth**—because depth doesn’t only demand breath-hold ability, it adds **pressure** and all the effects that come with it.

A key idea is that **apnea duration still matters in every discipline**, and you can extend it by increasing oxygen stores (lungs/blood), improving tolerance to low oxygen and high CO₂, and lowering oxygen use (better relaxation, efficiency, and technique). But in depth disciplines, those “classic” freediving limits sit inside a bigger box of pressure-related problems.

The review breaks down the main depth-specific stressors. First are **direct pressure effects**, especially on air spaces: ears, sinuses, and the lungs/chest (including the risk of lung squeeze and other barotrauma). Second are **gas effects under pressure**—the paper discusses how increased partial pressures can bring **narcotic-like effects** (impaired judgement, slower thinking, altered perception). That matters because depth freediving requires good decisions when you’re already under stress and running low on oxygen.

Then there’s the issue that many freedivers don’t like to think about: **decompression stress**. The paper highlights decompression sickness (and possibly air embolism during ascent) as risks that must be considered, especially as depths and total dive exposure increase. One important difference between pool disciplines and deep diving is that in depth you can’t always “abort instantly”—if something starts going wrong, you still have to travel back to the surface, and that delay increases the danger of severe hypoxia near the end of the dive.

Interestingly, the author notes that for many elite deep divers, pure breath-hold duration hasn’t been the main limiter *so far*—but as targets get deeper, it may require **exceptionally long apneas** and potentially **slower ascents** to reduce decompression risk. The review also suggests that narcotic effects could become part of the “ultimate depth limit,” and mentions that elite divers themselves have predicted future limits around the mid-100 m range for constant weight with fins.

Overall, the message is: pushing depth isn’t only about getting tougher. It’s about meeting multiple physiological challenges at once, using technique and equipment wisely, and developing safety procedures at the same pace as performance improves.

## Abstract
Part I described the physiological factors defining the limits of static apnea, while Part II examined performance in dynamic distance swimming. This paper reviews the factors determining performance in the depth disciplines, where hydrostatic pressure is added to the stressors associated with apnea duration and physical work. Apneic duration is essential for performance in all disciplines, and is prolonged by any means that increases gas storage or tolerance to asphyxia, or that reduces metabolic rate. For underwater distance swimming, the main challenge is to restrict metabolism despite the work of swimming, and to redirect blood flow to allow the most vital functions. Here, work economy, local tissue energy and oxygen stores, anaerobic capacity of the muscles, and possibly technical improvements will be essential for further development. In the depth disciplines, direct pressure effects causing barotrauma, the narcotic effects of gases, decompression sickness (DCS) and possibly air embolism during ascent need to be taken into account, as does the risk of hypoxia when the dive cannot be rapidly interrupted before the surface is reached again. While in most deep divers apneic duration is not the main limitation thus far, greater depths may call for exceptionally long apneas and slower ascents to avoid DCS. Narcotic effects may also affect the ultimate depth limit, which elite divers predict to be around 156 metres’ sea water for constant weight with fins. To reach these depths, serious physiological challenges have to be met, technical developments are likely to be needed and safety procedures must be developed concomitantly.
