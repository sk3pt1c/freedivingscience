## Why This Matters for Freedivers
This paper gives a simple, motivating message: some of your freediving “potential” is physical hardware—big lungs and a big, responsive spleen can genuinely help. But it also shows you don’t need perfect genetics to be elite, and it points to smart training goals: build lung capacity (safely) and use warm-ups/serial apneas to trigger the spleen response before a max attempt.

## Synopsis
Freedivers talk a lot about mindset, technique, and CO₂ tolerance—but this study asks a blunt question: do “bigger parts” actually predict better competitive performance? Specifically, the authors looked at two pieces of freediving hardware that can increase oxygen storage:

1) Lung volume (Vital Capacity, VC) — more air usually means more oxygen stored in the lungs.  
2) Spleen size — the spleen can contract during apneas and release extra red blood cells into circulation, temporarily increasing hemoglobin/hematocrit and improving oxygen carrying capacity.

### Who they studied
They tested 14 male elite freedivers who were competing at a world championship (average ~5.8 years of apnea training). These weren’t casual divers: their average performances were roughly 75 m constant weight, 5:53 static, and 139 m dynamic, which is extremely high.

### What they measured
- Spleen volume using ultrasound at rest (measuring length/width/thickness and calculating volume).  
- Vital capacity (VC) with spirometry (normal max inhale/exhale, no packing).  
- They then compared these to each diver’s total competition score (points from depth, static time, and dynamic distance).

### The main result: both spleen size and lung size predicted performance
The findings were surprisingly clean:
- Bigger spleens = higher competition scores.  
- Bigger vital capacity = higher competition scores.  

In other words, in this elite group, the divers who scored best tended to have both larger lungs and larger spleens.

### A concrete comparison: top scorers vs low scorers
The authors compared the top three scorers to the bottom three scorers:
- The top three had much larger spleens (around ~538 mL average) than the bottom three (around ~270 mL).  
- The top three also had larger vital capacity (about ~7.9 L vs ~6.7 L).

They then did a simple “oxygen accounting” estimate to translate those size differences into potential apnea benefits. Their rough calculation suggested:
- The spleen size difference could be worth about ~15 seconds of apnea advantage.  
- The lung volume difference could be worth about ~60 seconds of apnea advantage.  

This isn’t claiming you automatically gain exactly those seconds—but it’s a great way to understand scale: lungs look like a very big lever, and the spleen is a smaller but still meaningful lever, especially for repeated efforts and warm-ups.

### Does training explain it, or is it genetics
The paper can’t fully answer that. It raises a really interesting possibility: maybe successful divers self-select into the sport because they naturally have bigger lungs/spleens. But it also discusses evidence that lung volume can increase with certain training, and that spleen size might be capable of growth because it’s a tissue with strong regenerative ability.

### Practical takeaway
If you want to improve performance, this paper points toward two high-impact areas:
- Increase usable oxygen storage (lung capacity, safely and gradually; and techniques that help you use it efficiently).  
- Use warm-ups intelligently (serial apneas to trigger spleen contraction) before a max attempt.

It also reassures you: there was a wide spread in spleen and lung size even among world-class divers, so “hardware” helps—but it’s not the only thing that makes an elite freediver.

## Abstract
Humans share with seals the ability to contract the spleen and increase circulating hematocrit, which may improve apneic performance by enhancing gas storage. Seals have large spleens and while human spleen size is small in comparison, it shows great individual variation. Unlike many marine mammals, human divers rely to a great extent on lung oxygen stores, but the impact of lung volume on competitive apnea performance has never been determined. We studied if spleen- and lung size correlated with performance in elite apnea divers. Volunteers were 14 male apnea world championship participants, with a mean (SE) of 5.8 (1.2) years of previous apnea training. Spleen volume was calculated from spleen length, width, and thickness measured via ultrasound during rest, and vital capacity via spirometry. Accumulated competition scores from dives of maximal depth, time, and distance were compared to anthropometric measurements and training data. Mean (SE) diving performance was 75 (4) m for constant weight depth, 5 min 53 (39) s for static apnea and 139 (13) m for dynamic apnea distance. Subjects’ mean height was 184 (2) cm, weight 82 (3) kg, vital capacity (VC) 7.3 (0.3) L and spleen volume 336 (32) mL. Spleen volume did not correlate with subject height or weight, but was positively correlated with competition score (r = 0.57; P < 0.05). Total competition score was also positively correlated with VC (r = 0.54; P < 0.05). The three highest scoring divers had the greatest spleen volumes, averaging 538 (53) mL, while the three lowest-scoring divers had a volume of 270 (71) mL (P < 0.01). VC was also greater in the high-scorers, at 7.9 (0.36) L as compared to 6.7 (0.19) L in the low scorers (P < 0.01). Spleen volume was reduced to half after 2 min of apnea in the highest scoring divers, and the estimated resting apnea time gain from the difference between high and low scorers was 15 s for spleen volume and 60 s for VC. We conclude that both spleen- and lung volume predict apnea performance in elite divers.
