## Why This Matters for Freedivers
Even though this review is written for scuba, the *physics and the ear anatomy are the same* for freedivers. It’s one of the clearest, most practical “what goes wrong with ears in diving and what to do about it” summaries—useful for preventing injuries, spotting red flags early (especially vertigo and sudden hearing changes), and understanding when you should stop diving and get checked properly.

## Synopsis
This paper is a systematic review of diving-related ear problems (“otology”) with a strong practical aim: help clinicians and dive medicine specialists diagnose and manage the most common ear issues caused by pressure changes, water exposure, and (less commonly) decompression illness. The authors searched multiple medical databases up to early 2017 and pulled together dozens of papers spanning the **outer ear**, **middle ear**, and **inner ear**, plus related problems like motion sickness and “land sickness” after time at sea.

A big part of the review focuses on **barotrauma**, which is basically pressure injury. The middle ear is the classic trouble spot: on descent, the middle ear becomes “under-pressured” unless the Eustachian tube opens to equalize. If equalization fails and you keep going, tissue swelling and bleeding can occur, and in more severe cases the eardrum can rupture. The review makes a key point that many divers learn the hard way: the biggest pressure change is near the surface, so “it’s only a few meters” does not automatically mean “safe for the ears.”

It also covers why **head position and technique matter**. Equalizing tends to work better in a more head-up posture, and repeated forced Valsalva can be risky. The review discusses multiple equalization maneuvers and highlights that gentler, controlled methods are preferred. It also explains **alternobaric vertigo**—that sudden spinning feeling that can happen when one ear equalizes differently than the other, often during ascent. The practical advice is calm but important: stop your movement, stabilize, and let the sensation pass rather than reacting with panic.

For the outer ear, the review covers **otitis externa** (“swimmer’s ear”) as a very common diving issue, plus **exostoses** (bony growths in the ear canal, often from repeated cold water exposure). These don’t always stop you from diving, but they can raise the risk of infections, blockage, and unpleasant symptoms—so prevention and early management matter.

The inner ear section is the “high stakes” part: **inner ear barotrauma** and **inner ear decompression sickness** can both cause vertigo, hearing loss, tinnitus, and imbalance, and they can look similar at first. The review emphasizes careful history-taking (what happened on descent vs ascent, how equalization went, what the dive profile looked like) and taking sudden hearing/vertigo symptoms seriously. It also summarizes return-to-dive considerations after ear injuries and after certain ear surgeries (e.g., tubes, tympanoplasty, stapes surgery), which is useful for any diver trying to make safe decisions long-term.

Overall, it’s a wide-angle “field guide” style review: what problems happen most, what causes them, how to treat them, and how to think about fitness-to-dive decisions afterward.

## Abstract
Scuba diving is associated with a high incidence of head and neck injuries, most commonly affecting the outer, middle, and inner ear. This systematic review summarizes the pathophysiology, diagnosis, and treatment of diving-related otologic disorders, including barotrauma and decompression-related complications, and provides evidence-based recommendations where possible. The review also discusses related conditions such as seasickness, mal de debarquement, and fitness-to-dive considerations after otologic surgery.
