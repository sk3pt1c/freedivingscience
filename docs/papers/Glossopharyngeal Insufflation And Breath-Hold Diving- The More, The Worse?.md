## Why This Matters for Freedivers
This is one of the clearest “risk” papers on packing: it shows that the first seconds after packing can produce a sharp drop in blood pressure, cardiac output, and even brain blood flow—exactly the combination that can cause a sudden packing blackout. The practical takeaway is simple: the more you pack, the bigger the hit can be, so packing should be minimized and treated as an advanced tool with real safety consequences.

## Synopsis
Freedivers pack to get more air into the lungs than normal, mainly to make equalisation easier and to feel more comfortable going deep. But divers have also reported a nasty side effect: feeling dizzy—or even losing consciousness—right at the start of the breath-hold. This short report tackles that exact problem by comparing what happens during a breath-hold done normally at full inhale versus a breath-hold done after packing.

The authors followed two world-class competitive freedivers during two separate 4-minute apneas while lying on their backs: one apnea after a normal maximal inhale (100% vital capacity), and one apnea after packing. They measured heart rate, blood pressure, stroke volume and cardiac output, and they also tracked brain blood-flow velocity using transcranial Doppler (a practical proxy for changes in cerebral blood flow).

The key finding happens immediately: after packing, both divers showed a clear “hemodynamic dip” in the first seconds—cardiac output and blood pressure dropped, and the brain blood-flow signal dropped with them. In contrast, after a normal full inhale without packing, there was initially a short-lived boost in cardiac output (mainly from a faster heart rate) before the typical apnea pattern set in.

Even more interesting: the diver who packed more (bigger lung-volume increase) had a much more dramatic drop. In this report, diver 1 increased lung volume by about 22% with packing, and he showed the strongest crash in blood pressure and brain blood-flow velocity. Diver 2 packed less (about a 10% increase) and had a milder effect. That’s where the title comes from: **more packing, worse early instability**.

After the first minute or two, the differences between the “packed” and “non-packed” apneas became much smaller. In other words, packing’s biggest danger zone isn’t at the end of the breath-hold—it’s right at the beginning, when a sudden dip in circulation can lead to a fast, unexpected loss of consciousness.

The authors finish with practical recommendations: limit the amount/number of packing maneuvers, consider packing in a supine position (standing makes fainting more likely), aim for the minimum packing needed for comfort/equalisation, and make sure divers are closely watched at the beginning of apnea.

## Abstract
Competitive freedivers often use glossopharyngeal insufflation (lung packing), but there are reports of fainting or loss of consciousness in the first seconds of apnea. This brief report compares hemodynamic changes during apnea with and without packing in two world-class breath-hold divers. When packing was used, cardiac output, blood pressure, and cerebral blood-flow velocity dropped more sharply at the start of the breath-hold than during a normal full-inhale apnea. The diver who increased lung volume more showed the larger early disturbance. After the initial drop, the later time course of responses became more similar between conditions. The authors recommend limiting the maneuver and considering a supine position to reduce injury risk.
