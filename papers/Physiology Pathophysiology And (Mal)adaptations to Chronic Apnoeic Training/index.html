    Why This Matters for Freedivers

    This is a big-picture “map” of what science currently thinks makes breath-hold divers different — what changes with training, what might be genetic, and what’s still uncertain. If you’ve ever wondered why some people progress fast, why the diving reflex feels stronger over time, or why certain training blocks “work” (and sometimes backfire), this review ties those pieces together.

    It also flags the areas where performance and long-term health might clash. The authors summarize early warning signals from research on the brain, kidneys, bones, and the cardiovascular system — not to scare anyone, but to point out what should be monitored and what questions the freediving community still needs answered.

    Synopsis

    Freediving looks simple from the outside: one breath, then silence. But inside the body it’s a controlled crisis — oxygen steadily drops, carbon dioxide climbs, pressure rises with depth, and the nervous system tries to keep the brain alive while the urge to breathe ramps up. This review pulls together decades of findings to explain what limits a breath-hold, why divers vary so much, and what chronic apnea training seems to change over time.

    First, it breaks down the core ingredients of apnea performance: how much oxygen you can store at the start (lungs, blood, muscle), how efficiently you conserve and use that oxygen, and how well you tolerate the growing “breathing alarm” and involuntary diaphragm contractions. The review highlights the diving response (slowed heart rate and blood vessel tightening in the limbs) as a key survival tool — and notes that trained breath-hold divers usually show a stronger version of it than non-divers. In simple terms: with practice, your body gets better at prioritizing oxygen for the brain and heart.

    Next, it looks at “training adaptations” that may help performance. Elite divers often show changes in blood and muscle that support oxygen transport and usage — but the evidence isn’t always consistent across studies. For example, some training protocols appear to boost signals linked to making more red blood cells, while other studies find little or no meaningful long-term change. The authors make an important point here: a lot depends on the exact training style (static vs dynamic, how intense, how often), and some measurements can be misleading if they’re affected by fluid shifts rather than true increases in oxygen-carrying capacity.

    The review also covers stress and recovery: repeated apnea can increase oxidative stress (a burst of “reactive” molecules during re-oxygenation), but trained divers may handle this better, showing signs of stronger antioxidant defenses. It then shifts to the bigger, more uncomfortable question: what are the possible downsides of long-term exposure to extreme hypoxia and pressure? The authors summarize early findings that raise questions about neurocognition and brain imaging changes in some diver populations, as well as possible impacts on kidneys and bone health. At the same time, they emphasize that the long-term picture is not yet clear — we need better longitudinal studies that follow divers over years, not just snapshots after a session or season.

    Overall, this paper is less about “one magic trick” for longer breath-holds and more about understanding the whole system: performance traits, what training likely changes, what might be inherited, and what health signals deserve careful attention as the sport grows.

    Abstract

    Breath-hold diving is an activity that humans have engaged in since antiquity to forage for resources, provide sustenance and to support military campaigns. In modern times, breath-hold diving continues to gain popularity and recognition as both a competitive and recreational sport. The continued progression of world records is somewhat remarkable, particularly given the extreme hypoxaemic and hypercapnic conditions, and hydrostatic pressures these athletes endure. However, there is abundant literature to suggest a large inter-individual variation in the apnoeic capabilities that is thus far not fully understood. In this review, we explore developments in apnoea physiology and delineate the traits and mechanisms that potentially underpin this variation. In addition, we sought to highlight the physiological (mal)adaptations associated with consistent breath-hold training. Breath-hold divers (BHDs) are evidenced to exhibit a more pronounced diving-response than non-divers, while elite BHDs (EBHDs) also display beneficial adaptations in both blood and skeletal muscle. Importantly, these physiological characteristics are documented to be primarily influenced by training-induced stimuli. BHDs are exposed to unique physiological and environmental stressors, and as such possess an ability to withstand acute cerebrovascular and neuronal strains. Whether these characteristics are also a result of training-induced adaptations or genetic predisposition is less certain. Although the long-term effects of regular breath-hold diving activity are yet to be holistically established, preliminary evidence has posed considerations for cognitive, neurological, renal and bone health in BHDs. These areas should be explored further in longitudinal studies to more confidently ascertain the long-term health implications of extreme breath-holding activity.
